NOTE 01 / Foundations

Think in threats. Work in evidence.

2 MIN READEDITED 30 OCT 2026

A useful security note connects a threat to something valuable, a failure that could expose it, and an observation that could reveal the failure.

Start with the asset

The early notebook entries collect ransomware resources and malware histories. The useful next step is to turn each topic into questions: what could be lost, how could that happen, and what evidence would distinguish an incident from ordinary failure?

Use a fictional document service as a working example. Its assets include documents, access permissions, service availability, and the ability to restore a known state. A stolen account could expose documents without installing malware. A broken deployment could interrupt service without any attacker. Describe the effect before assigning a cause.

QuestionExample observationNext check
Is availability affected?Users cannot retrieve documents.Compare health checks, errors, and recent changes.
Has access changed?An account has a new privileged role.Check the approval and identity audit trail.
Can the service recover?A restore exercise fails.Inspect backup coverage and recovery dependencies.

Organize the response

NIST SP 800-61r3 places incident response within wider cybersecurity risk management. Preparation and improvement connect to detection, response, and recovery; the work starts before an alert arrives. Use that structure to identify owners, escalation paths, necessary records, and recovery expectations. [1]

For a tabletop exercise, write a timeline with a timezone. Record the observation, its source, the decision, the decision owner, and the reason. Preserve the original record separately from an interpretation. If the evidence changes, update the interpretation.

Practice: a service becomes unavailable

  1. Invent three competing explanations: a failed deployment, an infrastructure outage, and unauthorized activity.
  2. For each, name one observation that would support it and one that would weaken it.
  3. Choose the first read-only check that separates those explanations.
  4. Write the condition under which the response owner would escalate or authorize containment.

This is a proposed exercise, not an account of a completed incident. Its output is a one-page reasoning record, not a diagnosis made from an alert title.

Notebook to article

Historical malware names and incident bookmarks were retained as research prompts. Old claims about attribution and events happening “in the past year” were not republished as current facts.
